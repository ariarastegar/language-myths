Inuit and Yupik languages are polysynthetic: suffixes stack onto a root, so one 'word' can mean 'it's snowing hard again apparently'. Count every form and you get a near-infinite number of snow words, and the same for every other noun.

Count roots instead of forms and you get a real but finite number. Franz Boas originally cited just four unrelated words, and a 1927 West Greenlandic dictionary gives two basic roots: one for snow in the air and one for snow on the ground.

## Caveats

- **There really is a rich vocabulary.** A 2010 survey of about ten Inuit and Yupik dialects found around 40 snow terms in Central Siberian Yupik and at least 53 in Nunavik Inuit, with sea-ice vocabulary richer still. Debunkers who reduce it to 'only two words' overcorrect.
- **The real lesson is about the myth.** Laura Martin showed in 1986 how the numbers grew as the claim was retold, and Geoffrey Pullum's 1989 column 'The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax' made her point famous.

## Examples

- Ask how many English words for 'house' there are if *houses, housing, rehoused, houseboat* all count.
