What stopped was a written standard: Classical Latin stopped being anyone's mother tongue. The spoken Latin underneath kept going and kept changing, and it diverged region by region until the local forms got their own names.

There was no day on which anyone stopped speaking Latin and started speaking Italian. What was eventually recognized was that the spoken language and the written one had grown too far apart.

## Caveats

- **Some branches really did die.** Dalmatian died out when its last speaker died in 1898. Lineages do end, and that's what language death actually looks like.
- **'Dead language' is a fair label for the standard.** Classical Latin as a fixed written variety has no native speakers. The myth is only the idea that the lineage ended.

## Examples

- **Council of Tours, 813:** priests were told to preach in the 'rustic Roman tongue' so that people could understand them. That shows the gap was already being felt.
- **Strasbourg Oaths, 842:** often cited as the first text written in something recognizably French.
